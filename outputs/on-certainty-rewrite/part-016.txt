**351.** The question "Do these words have meaning?" resembles the question "Is that a tool?" where one holds up, say, a hammer. One may reply: "Yes, it is a hammer." But what if the object that most of us would classify as a hammer functions elsewhere as a projectile or as a baton for directing an orchestra? The application to language should now be evident.

**352.** If someone declares "I know that that's a tree," one might respond: "Yes, that is a sentence—an English sentence. But what purpose is it serving?" Suppose the speaker answers: "I merely wished to remind myself that I am acquainted with such things."

**353.** But suppose instead the speaker said: "I intend to make a logical observation." Consider a forester who enters a wood with his workers and says "This tree must be cut down, and this one, and this one." What if he then remarks "I know that that's a tree"? Yet might we not say of this forester: "He knows that that's a tree—he doesn't examine it, nor does he instruct his workers to examine it"?

**354.** There is behavior characteristic of doubting and behavior characteristic of not doubting. The former exists only where the latter exists.

**355.** A psychiatrist might ask me "Do you know what that is?" and I might answer "I know that it's a chair; I recognize it; it has always been in my room." He poses this question, perhaps, not to test my vision but to test my capacity to recognize objects, to know their designations and their functions. What is at issue is a form of knowing one's way about. Now it would be incorrect for me to say "I believe that it's a chair," for that would express my willingness to have my statement tested. Whereas "I know that it is a chair" carries the implication that I would be bewildered if what I stated were not confirmed.

**356.** My mental state—the "knowing"—affords me no guarantee concerning what will occur. But it consists in this: I would not understand where a doubt could gain a foothold, nor where a further test would be possible.

**357.** One might put it thus: "I know" expresses a comfortable certainty, not a certainty that is still contending with uncertainty.

**358.** I would like to regard this certainty not as something resembling hastiness or superficiality, but as a form of life. (This formulation is poorly expressed and probably poorly conceived as well.)

**359.** But this means that I wish to conceive of it as something that lies beyond justification or lack of justification—as something, one might say, that is animal in nature.

**360.** I know that this is my foot. I could not accept any experience as evidence to the contrary. This may function as an exclamation; but what follows from it? At a minimum, that I will act with a certainty that admits no doubt, in accordance with my conviction.

**361.** Yet I might also say: It has been revealed to me by God that it is so. God has instructed me that this is my foot. In that case, if anything occurred that appeared to conflict with this knowledge, I would have to regard that occurrence as deception.

**362.** But doesn't it emerge here that knowledge is related to a decision?

**363.** And here it is difficult to locate the transition from the exclamation one feels compelled to make to its consequences in one's conduct.

**364.** One might also pose this question: "If you know that that is your foot, do you also know—or do you merely believe—that no future experience will appear to contradict your knowledge?" (That is, that nothing will appear to you yourself to do so.)

**365.** If someone replied: "I also know that it will never appear to me as though anything contradicted that knowledge"—what could we infer from that, apart from the fact that he himself entertains no doubt that it will never happen?

**366.** Suppose it were prohibited to say "I know" and one were permitted only to say "I believe I know."

**367.** Isn't the purpose of construing a word like "know" analogously to "believe" precisely that opprobrium then attaches to the statement "I know" if the person making it is mistaken? The consequence is that error becomes something forbidden.

**368.** If someone declares that he will recognize no experience as evidence for the opposite, that declaration is, after all, a decision. It remains possible that he will act contrary to it.

**369.** If I wished to doubt whether this were my hand, how could I avoid doubting whether the word "hand" has any meaning? This suggests that there is something I do know after all.

**370.** But more accurately: The fact that I employ the word "hand" and all the other words in my sentence without hesitation—indeed, that I would find myself before an abyss if I attempted so much as to doubt their meanings—shows that absence of doubt belongs to the essence of the language-game. The question "How do I know...?" either displaces the language-game or else abolishes it.

**371.** Doesn't "I know that that's a hand," in Moore's sense, mean the same thing, or approximately the same thing, as: I can make statements such as "I have a pain in this hand" or "This hand is weaker than the other" or "I once broke this hand," along with countless others, within language-games where doubt concerning the existence of this hand does not arise?

**372.** Only in particular cases is it possible to conduct an investigation into "Is that really a hand?" (or "my hand"). For "I doubt whether that is really my (or a) hand" lacks sense without some more precise specification. One cannot determine from these words alone whether any doubt is meant at all—nor what sort of doubt is at issue.

**373.** Why should it be possible to possess grounds for believing something if it is not possible to be certain?

**374.** We teach a child "That is your hand," not "That is perhaps [or 'probably'] your hand." This is how a child learns the innumerable language-games that concern his hand. An investigation or question as to whether this is really a hand never occurs to him. Nor, conversely, does he learn that he knows that this is a hand.

**375.** Here one must recognize that complete absence of doubt at some juncture—even where we would say that legitimate doubt could exist—need not render a language-game defective. For there exists something like an alternative arithmetic as well. This admission, I believe, must underlie any comprehension of logic.

**376.** If someone declares "I know what that is—it's a chair" when the chair stands in his room, this sentence possesses meaning because it can be tested. But would it possess the same meaning if I were pointing at something no one could doubt, if the context rendered testing impossible?

**377.** The language-game of reporting can be given such a turn that a report is not meant to inform the recipient, but rather the one making it.

**378.** But suppose now that a person who has always lived in a particular environment, without ever leaving it, goes to a different place, where the rules are quite different. Then if someone says to him "This is a tree," it would perhaps not be clear what would be the sense of such a proposition. For the word might have a quite different use in that new environment. So whether what was said makes sense depends on the setting, on the language-game in which it figures.

**379.** I might also say: We do not always need to investigate our propositions. But can we understand what it would be like for our propositions to require investigation? If I do not know whether this is my hand, would I know whether the word "hand" means anything?

**380.** A language-game: Report whether a certain body has such-and-such a color. But doubts are not part of the game. If I doubt, I do not know what I am supposed to doubt. The game presupposes that certain things are beyond doubt.

**381.** One might say: "The proposition 'I know that that's a tree' if it was said in quite unphilosophical surroundings, would be intended to convince another that I am indeed familiar with this object, not that the object possesses such-and-such qualities."

**382.** That is, the issue is whether the doubt being expressed makes sense in the first instance.

**383.** The argument "I may be dreaming" is senseless for this reason: if I am dreaming, this remark is being dreamed as well—and indeed it is also being dreamed that these words have any meaning.

**384.** Justification by experience comes to an end. If it did not it would not be justification.

**385.** Could someone be taught that he knows that that is a chair? Such instruction would presuppose that he can employ the concept of a chair; that he has already learned what a chair is.
