It is rather as if the child were inquiring whether some mountain is taller than a particular house he has seen. To respond to such a question, I would be conveying to the questioner an entire framework concerning the world's structure.

If I answer such a question with certainty, what is the source of this certainty?

234. I believe that I have ancestors, and that every human being has them. I believe that various cities exist, and, quite generally, the principal facts of geography and history. I believe that the earth is a body upon whose surface we move and that it no more suddenly vanishes or undergoes similar transformations than any other solid body: this table, this house, this tree, and so forth. If I were to doubt the earth's existence long before my birth, I would have to doubt all manner of things that stand fast for me.

235. And that something stands fast for me is not grounded in my stupidity or credulity.

That I do not doubt certain propositions reflects not some cognitive limitation but rather their functional role. These propositions stand fast not because I have examined and confirmed them, but because they constitute the framework within which examination and confirmation proceed.

236. If someone were to say "The earth has not long existed," what would he be challenging? Do I know?

Would what he challenges necessarily be what is called a scientific belief? Might it not be a mystical conviction? Is there any absolute necessity that he be contradicting historical facts? Or even geographical ones?

The answer depends on what role the challenged proposition plays in the speaker's network of beliefs. The proposition "The earth has existed for a very long time" might function as an empirical claim subject to geological evidence, or as something closer to a framework principle, or as a religious or metaphysical commitment. The same words can occupy different logical positions.

237. If I say "An hour ago this table did not exist," I probably mean that it was constructed later.

If I say "This mountain did not exist then," I presumably mean that it formed later—perhaps through volcanic activity.

If I say "This mountain did not exist half an hour ago," that is such a strange statement that it remains unclear what I mean. Perhaps I mean something false but scientific in character. You might think the statement that the mountain did not exist then is entirely clear, regardless of how one conceives the context. But suppose someone said: "This mountain did not exist a minute ago, but an exactly similar one existed in its place instead." Only the familiar context allows what is meant to emerge clearly.

What makes the first two statements intelligible is that they fit recognized patterns of discourse. Tables are manufactured; mountains form through geological processes. The third statement resists interpretation because it invokes a time-scale—half an hour—that fits no recognized pattern for mountains coming into existence. The fourth statement lies so far outside any framework we employ that we cannot determine whether it is false, confused, or employing words in some unanticipated technical sense.

238. I might therefore question someone who claimed that the earth did not exist before his birth, in order to discover which of my convictions he was at odds with. And then it might emerge that he was contradicting fundamental attitudes of mine, and that this was simply how matters stood, and that I would have to accept it.

Similarly if he said he had at some time been on the moon.

I would need to determine what conviction of mine stands opposed to his assertion. Perhaps he employs "birth" in an unusual sense. Perhaps his cosmology differs fundamentally from mine. Perhaps he is stating something mystical rather than empirical. Until I understand which of my beliefs his statement contradicts, I cannot assess the nature of our disagreement.

239. I believe that every human being has two human parents; but Catholics believe that Jesus had only a human mother. And other people might believe that there are human beings with no parents, and give no credence to all the contrary evidence. Catholics believe as well that under certain circumstances a wafer completely changes its nature, while all evidence demonstrates the contrary. And so if Moore said "I know that this is wine and not blood," Catholics would contradict him.

This shows that what stands fast for one person may not stand fast for another. The conviction that every human has two biological parents functions for me as something like a framework principle—not because I have verified it in every instance, but because it organizes a vast amount of what I believe about human reproduction, heredity, anatomy, and physiology. Yet for others, this same proposition functions as an empirical claim that admits of exceptions. The difference lies not necessarily in our evidence but in the role the proposition plays in our respective systems of belief.

240. On what is the belief that all human beings have parents based? On experience. And how can I base this certain belief on my experience? Well, I base it not only on the fact that I have known the parents of certain people but on everything I have learned about the sexual life of human beings and their anatomy and physiology: also on what I have heard and seen of animals. But then is that really a proof?

I call my belief certain, yet the experience on which it allegedly rests does not amount to a proof. I have not observed the parents of every human being. The certainty I attribute to the belief seems to exceed what this inductive base could warrant.

241. Is this not rather a hypothesis which, as I believe, is again and again completely confirmed?

This suggestion treats the belief as an empirical generalization repeatedly verified by observation. But this characterization fits uneasily with the certainty I attribute to it.

242. Must we not say at every turn: "I believe this with certainty"?

If our certainty requires constant reassertion, this suggests it lacks the character of knowledge.

243. One says "I know" when one is ready to give compelling grounds. "I know" relates to a possibility of demonstrating the truth. Whether someone knows something can come to light, assuming that he is convinced of it.

But if what he believes is of such a kind that the grounds he can give are no surer than his assertion, then he cannot say that he knows what he believes.

This establishes a constraint on knowledge-claims: one knows only when one can provide grounds more certain than the assertion itself. If the best grounds I can offer for "I have two hands" are no more certain than the proposition itself, I cannot claim to know it—not because the proposition is uncertain, but because it occupies a logical position where the concept of knowledge fails to gain purchase.

244. If someone says "I have a body," he can be asked "Who is speaking here with this mouth?"

The question exposes the peculiarity of the assertion. To ask who speaks is already to presuppose that someone with a body speaks. The claim "I have a body" thus purports to assert what is already presupposed in its assertion.

245. To whom does anyone say that he knows something? To himself, or to someone else. If he says it to himself, how is it distinguished from the assertion that he is certain that things are thus? There is no subjective certainty that I know something. The certainty is subjective, but not the knowledge. So if I say "I know that I have two hands," and that is not supposed to express merely my subjective certainty, I must be able to satisfy myself that I am right. But I cannot do that, for my having two hands is not less certain before I have looked at them than afterwards. But I could say: "That I have two hands is an irreversible belief." That would express the fact that I am not ready to let anything count as disproof of this proposition.

The distinction between subjective certainty and knowledge becomes crucial. Knowledge is not merely a subjective state; it involves objective justification. Yet propositions like "I have two hands" resist this structure. Looking at my hands does not increase the certainty of the belief—which suggests that the belief's certainty does not derive from evidential justification. What characterizes such beliefs is rather their irreversibility: I am not prepared to count anything as evidence against them.

246. "Here I have arrived at a foundation of all my beliefs." "This position I will hold!" But is that not precisely only because I am completely convinced of it? What is 'being completely convinced' like?

If the foundation of my beliefs rests on nothing more than complete conviction, we must understand what this conviction consists in. It cannot be merely a strong subjective feeling, for that would render the foundation arbitrary.

247. What would it be like to doubt now whether I have two hands? Why can I not imagine it at all? What would I believe if I did not believe that? So far I have no system at all within which this doubt might exist.

The impossibility of doubt here is not psychological but logical. To doubt whether I have two hands, I would need some alternative hypothesis, some framework within which the doubt could be situated. But I possess no such framework. The belief that I have two hands is not one element within a system where alternatives are conceived—it is part of what structures the system itself.

248. I have arrived at the rock bottom of my convictions.

And one might almost say that these foundation-walls are carried by the whole house.

This image inverts the usual architectural metaphor. Ordinarily, foundations support the structure built upon them. But here, the foundational beliefs are sustained by the entire edifice of beliefs they ostensibly support. The relationship is not one-directional but systemic: the foundations and the structure mutually support one another.

249. One gives oneself a false picture of doubt.

We imagine doubt as something we could direct toward any proposition simply by withholding assent. But genuine doubt requires a background of certainty.

250. My having two hands is, in normal circumstances, as certain as anything I could produce in evidence for it.

That is why I am not in a position to take the sight of my hand as evidence for it.

The seeing does not stand to the belief as evidence to conclusion. If it did, the seeing would need to be more certain than the belief. But it is not.

251. Does this not mean: I shall proceed according to this belief unconditionally, and not let anything confuse me?

This captures part of what it means for something to stand fast. I treat the belief as beyond revision in my practical reasoning.

252. But it is not merely that I believe in this way that I have two hands, but that every reasonable person does.

The irreversibility is not idiosyncratic but shared. This suggests that what stands fast does so not as a matter of individual psychology but as part of what defines reasonable judgment within a community.

253. At the foundation of well-founded belief lies belief that is not founded.

This does not mean that foundational beliefs are arbitrary or irrational. It means rather that they do not derive their status from being grounded in further beliefs. They function as grounds rather than as grounded.

254. Any 'reasonable' person behaves like this.

Reasonableness itself presupposes certain beliefs that stand fast. One cannot first be reasonable and then adopt these beliefs; rather, holding these beliefs partly constitutes what it is to be reasonable.
