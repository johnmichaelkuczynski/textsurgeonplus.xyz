**202.** Moore's certain propositions function almost as declarations of a right—the right to rely upon certain forms of evidence. They announce what we may legitimately take as grounds.

**203.** Everything we recognize as evidence points toward the conclusion that the earth existed long before my birth. The contrary hypothesis finds no confirmatory support whatsoever.

When everything speaks for a hypothesis and nothing against it, does this constitute objective certainty? We may apply that label if we wish. But does such convergence of evidence necessarily establish agreement with the facts? At most, it reveals what we mean by "agreement." We find it hard to imagine the hypothesis false, yet also hard to specify what practical use we would make of it.

In what does this agreement consist, if not in the fact that what functions as evidence within our language-games supports our proposition? The notion of agreement gains its content from the evidential practices we already engage in.

**204.** The activity of giving grounds, of justifying our evidence, reaches a terminus. But this endpoint is not a matter of certain propositions striking us immediately as true—not a kind of intellectual seeing on our part. Rather, our acting forms the foundation of the language-game. Justification rests ultimately on what we do, not on what we perceive as self-evident.

**205.** If we define the true as that which is grounded, then the ground itself is neither true nor false. It occupies a different logical position from the propositions it grounds.

**206.** If someone asked us "but is that true?" we might answer affirmatively. If he then demanded grounds, we might reply: "I cannot provide you with grounds, but if you continue learning you too will adopt the same view."

If this convergence failed to occur, it would indicate that he could not, for instance, learn history. Certain background convictions constitute prerequisites for particular domains of learning.

**207.** "Strange coincidence, that every person whose skull has been opened had a brain!" The remark's ironic quality points to something about the status of such propositions. We do not treat them as empirical discoveries requiring confirmation through repeated observation.

**208.** I conduct a telephone conversation with New York. My friend reports that his young trees have buds of such and such a kind. I am thereby convinced that his tree possesses these characteristics. Am I also convinced that the earth exists?

The question seems misplaced. My conviction about the tree presupposes, rather than includes as a separate conviction, the earth's existence.

**209.** The existence of the earth belongs to the whole picture that forms the starting-point of belief for me. It does not figure as one belief among others within that picture, but rather as part of the framework that makes particular beliefs possible.

**210.** Does my telephone call to New York strengthen my conviction that the earth exists?

Much appears fixed, removed from the traffic of doubt and confirmation. It is shunted, so to speak, onto an unused siding—not because we have verified it exhaustively, but because it does not function as something subject to this kind of verification.

**211.** Such propositions give our way of looking at things, and our investigations, their form. Perhaps one or another was once disputed. But perhaps, for unthinkable ages, it has belonged to the scaffolding of our thought. Every human being has parents—this proposition operates as part of the framework, not as a hypothesis we test.

**212.** Under certain circumstances, we regard a calculation as sufficiently checked. What gives us a right to do so? Experience? May experience not have deceived us? Somewhere we must finish with justification, and then there remains simply the proposition that this is how we calculate. Our practice itself constitutes the bedrock, not some further justification for that practice.

**213.** Our empirical propositions do not form a homogeneous mass. They differ in logical status, in their relation to doubt and evidence, in the role they play within our system of beliefs.

**214.** What prevents me from supposing that this table either vanishes or alters its shape and color when no one observes it, then reverts to its former condition when someone looks at it again? "But who is going to suppose such a thing!"—one feels compelled to say.

The natural response reveals something important: we do not refrain from such suppositions because we have evidence against them, but because the supposition itself stands outside what we treat as coherent hypothesis.

**215.** Here we see that the idea of "agreement with reality" lacks any clear application. What would count as checking whether our fundamental framework agrees with reality, when that framework determines what we mean by reality and agreement?

**216.** The proposition "It is written"—its certainty derives from its role, from what we do with it, not from verification.

**217.** If someone supposed that all our calculations were uncertain and that we could rely on none of them, justifying himself by noting that mistakes are always possible, perhaps we would call him crazy. But can we say he is in error? Does he not simply react differently? We rely on calculations; he does not. We are sure; he is not.

The divergence appears not as disagreement about facts but as difference in form of life, in fundamental orientation toward a practice.

**218.** Can I believe for one moment that I have ever been in the stratosphere? No. So do I know the contrary, in Moore's sense?

**219.** There cannot be any doubt about it for me as a reasonable person. That's it—the matter terminates there, not in further justification but in what reasonableness requires.

**220.** The reasonable person does not entertain certain doubts. Reasonableness itself consists partly in recognizing which doubts make sense and which do not.

**221.** I cannot possibly doubt that I was never in the stratosphere. Does that constitute knowing it? Does it make it true?

**222.** For might I not be crazy and failing to doubt what I absolutely ought to doubt? The possibility of madness threatens to undermine the appeal to what reasonable people doubt, suggesting that our certainty might be pathological rather than justified.

**223.** But what distinguishes the reasonable person from the crazy one, if not precisely this pattern of doubt and certainty? We cannot step outside all our convictions to assess whether they collectively constitute sanity or madness.

**224.** "I know that it never happened, for if it had happened I could not possibly have forgotten it."

But supposing it did happen, then it would simply be the case that you had forgotten it. And how do you know that you could not possibly have forgotten it? Is that not itself derived from earlier experience—from cases where you remember what you would expect to remember?

**225.** What I hold fast to is not one proposition but a nest of propositions. They support one another, forming a system rather than standing independently.

**226.** Can I give the supposition that I have ever been on the moon any serious consideration at all? The question is not whether I have evidence against it, but whether the supposition achieves coherence within my framework of convictions.

**227.** "Is that something one can forget?!" The exclamation marks the proposition's status: certain things cannot intelligibly be supposed forgotten, not because our memory is perfect, but because forgetting them would require conditions we cannot coherently describe.

**228.** "In such circumstances, people do not say 'Perhaps we have all forgotten,' and the like, but rather they assume that..." Our shared practices determine what suppositions are entertained and what are set aside as incoherent.

**229.** Our talk gets its meaning from the rest of our proceedings. Utterances do not carry meaning independently of the activities and practices they are embedded in.

**230.** We are asking ourselves: what do we do with a statement "I know..."? For the question concerns not mental processes or mental states, but the role such statements play in our practices.

That is how one must decide whether something constitutes knowledge or not—by examining its function, not by introspecting a mental condition.

**231.** If someone doubted whether the earth had existed a hundred years ago, I should not understand, for this reason: I would not know what such a person would still allow to count as evidence and what not. The doubt would undermine the very framework within which evidence operates.

**232.** "We could doubt every single one of these facts, but we could not doubt them all."

Would it not be more accurate to say: "we do not doubt them all"? Our not doubting them constitutes our manner of judging, and therefore of acting. The impossibility is practical, not logical.

**233.** If a child asked me whether the earth existed before my birth, I should answer that the earth did not begin only with my birth, but existed long before. And I should have the feeling of saying something funny—not because the answer is uncertain, but because the question treats as open what functions as closed, as requiring confirmation what operates as framework.
