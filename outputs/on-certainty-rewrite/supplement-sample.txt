The movement from sections 10 through 13 traces a subtle but crucial progression in Wittgenstein's treatment of knowledge claims, one that requires careful attention to understand how his argument develops. The transition depends on recognizing that what appears to be nonsense in one context becomes intelligible in another, not because the words change their meaning intrinsically, but because the language-game in which they participate shifts. This principle, introduced through the comparison with "2 × 2 = 4," serves as the key to understanding why Moore's knowledge claims seem simultaneously obvious and problematic.

When Wittgenstein moves from the sick man example to the arithmetic proposition, he is not merely offering an analogy. Rather, he is demonstrating that sense itself is use-dependent across different categories of propositions. The arithmetic example establishes that even paradigmatically certain propositions derive their sense from their employment in practice. The same sentence might be nonsense in Chinese, not because arithmetic truths are contingent, but because the physical marks only become a proposition through their role in a practice. This prepares us to see that "I know that there's a sick man lying here" similarly depends on context, though in a different way. The proposition is not senseless in itself but becomes idle when uttered in circumstances where doubt has no application.

The crucial distinction emerges in section 11's claim about specialization. The use of "I know" is not merely context-sensitive in the way that pronouns require context; it is specialized in that it performs a particular function within epistemic practices. This function requires contrast with doubt or error to operate properly. When Wittgenstein says we fail to see this specialization, he means we treat "I know" as though it simply reports an internal state that obtains whenever we are not actually doubting. But section 12 immediately reveals why this picture is tempting: "I know" appears to describe a state of affairs that guarantees its own truth. The grammatical form suggests that knowing is a mental condition that makes error impossible.

The reference to "I thought I knew" serves as a corrective to this picture. This expression reminds us that claims to knowledge can be mistaken, which would be impossible if "I know" genuinely described an infallible mental state. This sets up the critical distinction in section 13 between first-person and third-person uses of knowledge claims. From another person's utterance "I know it is so," I cannot infer that it is so, even if the person is sincere. This shows that the utterance does not establish the fact known. However, Wittgenstein allows that from my own utterance I can infer the fact, answering his own question with "Yes." This affirmation is not a retreat to Cartesian certainty but rather an acknowledgment that when I properly claim to know something, the utterance and the fact stand in a particular logical relationship. The contrast between what follows from another's knowledge claim and what follows from my own points toward the need to establish objectively that one's claim to knowledge is warranted, a theme the subsequent sections develop by examining what such establishment requires.
