These materials derive from the final year and a half of Wittgenstein's life. During mid-1949, he accepted Norman Malcolm's invitation to visit the United States, residing at Malcolm's residence in Ithaca. Malcolm served as a catalyst for Wittgenstein's engagement with Moore's defense of common sense—specifically, Moore's assertion that he knows certain propositions with certainty, propositions such as "Here is one hand, and here is another," "The earth existed for a long time before my birth," and "I have never been far from the earth's surface." The first proposition appears in Moore's "Proof of the External World." The remaining two appear in his "Defence of Common Sense." Wittgenstein had maintained a longstanding interest in these claims and had informed Moore that this was his finest article. Moore had concurred with this assessment. This volume contains everything Wittgenstein wrote on this subject from that time until his death. The material is entirely first-draft, which he did not survive to select from and refine.

The material divides into four parts. We have indicated the divisions at §65, page 10; §192, page 27; and §299, page 38. What we take to be the first part was written on twenty loose sheets of lined foolscap, bearing no dates. Wittgenstein left these sheets in his room at G. E. M. Anscombe's house in Oxford, where he lived from April 1950 to February 1951, apart from an autumn visit to Norway. I (G. E. M. A.) retain the impression that he had written them in Vienna, where he stayed from the previous Christmas until March, but I cannot now recall the basis for this impression. The remainder appears in small notebooks containing dates. Toward the end, the date of writing is consistently given. The final entry was made two days before his death on April 29th, 1951. We have preserved the dates exactly as they appear in the manuscripts. The numbering of the individual sections, however, is the work of the editors.

These were not the only materials Wittgenstein wrote during this period. He wrote, among other things, a substantial amount on color-concepts, and this material he did excerpt and refine, reducing it to a small compass. We expect to publish a volume containing this material and other material written after the completion of Philosophical Investigations Part II. It seemed appropriate to publish this work separately. It is not a selection. Wittgenstein marked it off in his notebooks as a separate topic, which he apparently took up at four separate periods during these eighteen months. It constitutes a single sustained treatment of the subject.

G. E. M. Anscombe

1. If you know that here is one hand, we'll grant you all the rest. When one says that such and such a proposition cannot be proved, this does not mean that it cannot be derived from other propositions. Any proposition can be derived from other propositions. But the propositions from which it is derived may be no more certain than it is itself. (On this, a curious remark by H. Newman.)

2. From its seeming to me—or to everyone—to be so, it does not follow that it is so. What we can ask is whether it can make sense to doubt it.

3. Suppose someone says "I don't know if there's a hand here." He might be told "Look closer." This possibility of satisfying oneself is part of the language-game. It is one of the language-game's essential features.

4. "I know that I am a human being." To see how unclear the sense of this proposition is, consider its negation. At most, it might be taken to mean "I know I have the organs of a human"—for example, a brain, which no one has ever yet seen. But what about such a proposition as "I know I have a brain"? Can I doubt it? Grounds for doubt are lacking. Everything speaks in its favor; nothing speaks against it. Nevertheless, it is imaginable that my skull should turn out empty when it was operated on.

5. Whether a proposition can turn out false depends on what I make count as determinants for that proposition.

6. Now, can one enumerate what one knows, in the way Moore does? Straight off like that, I believe not. For otherwise the expression "I know" gets misused. And through this misuse, a queer and extremely important mental state seems to be revealed.

7. My life shows that I know or am certain that there is a chair over there, or a door, and so on. I tell a friend, for example, "Take that chair over there," "Shut the door," and so on.

8. The difference between the concept of knowing and the concept of being certain is not of any great importance at all, except where "I know" is meant to mean: I cannot be wrong. In a law-court, for example, "I am certain" could replace "I know" in every piece of testimony. We might even imagine its being forbidden to say "I know" there. (A passage in Wilhelm Meister uses "You know" or "You knew" in the sense "You were certain," the facts being different from what he knew.)

9. Now do I, in the course of my life, make sure I know that here is a hand—my own hand, that is?

10. "I know that a sick man is lying here"? Nonsense! I am sitting at his bedside; I am looking attentively into his face. So I don't know, then, that there is a sick man lying here? Neither the question nor the assertion makes sense. Any more than the assertion "I am here" makes sense, though I might yet use it at any moment if suitable occasion presented itself. Then is "2 × 2 = 4" nonsense in the same way, and not a proposition of arithmetic, apart from particular occasions? "2 × 2 = 4" is a true proposition of arithmetic—not "on particular occasions" nor "always"—but the spoken or written sentence "2 × 2 = 4" in Chinese might have a different meaning or be outright nonsense. From this it is seen that it is only in use that the proposition has its sense. And "I know that there's a sick man lying here," used in an unsuitable situation, seems not to be nonsense but rather seems matter-of-course. This is only because one can fairly easily imagine a situation to fit it, and one thinks that the words "I know that..." are always in place where there is no doubt, and hence even where the expression of doubt would be unintelligible.

11. We just do not see how very specialized the use of "I know" is.

12. For "I know" seems to describe a state of affairs which guarantees what is known—guarantees it as a fact. One always forgets the expression "I thought I knew."

13. For it is not as though the proposition "It is so" could be inferred from someone else's utterance: "I know it is so." Nor from the utterance together with its not being a lie. But can't I infer "It is so" from my own utterance "I know etc."? Yes; and also "There is a hand there" from the utterance "He knows that there's a hand there." But from his utterance "I know..." it does not follow that he does know it. The same holds for my own case. From what do we conclude that someone knows? From behavior and utterance together—and these do not yield certainty. The first-person case seems privileged because when I say "I know p" I am inclined to conclude p directly. But the warrant is the same: my utterance expresses belief, not fact.

14. That I am a man and not a woman can be verified, but if I were to say I know it, that could only signify: I know what the word "man" means. And this: that I am a man among others who can verify it. Everything descriptive of a physical object can also serve as check on whether I am making an error.

15. There are cases such that, if someone gives signs of doubt where we do not doubt, we cannot confidently understand his signs as signs of doubt. That is to say: if I am supposed to grant that a man can be in doubt about whether he has a body, then I must equally grant the same thing of everyone else, and must suppose they're frequently in doubt about it. If someone doubts whether he has a body, do we take this as genuine doubt? Or must we conclude he misunderstands the words? The language-game of doubting presupposes shared certainties. Where these are absent, interpretation cannot proceed.

16. "If I don't trust this evidence why should I trust any evidence?"

17. If I say "Of course I know that that's a towel" I am making an utterance. I have no thought of a verification. For me it is an immediate utterance. I don't think of past or future verification. The knowledge-claim functions as reaction, not as conclusion from evidence. This immediacy characterizes ordinary knowledge-claims. They are not based on anything in the sense of inference from grounds.

18. Suppose someone doubted whether I have two hands? Must I grant at least this: that I need not be certain? That my knowledge here is of special kind? The doubt, if coherent, would force acknowledgment that my "knowledge" of my hands differs from knowledge of yesterday's weather. But differs how? Not in certainty-degree. Rather: grounds for doubt and verification operate differently. With hands: what would checking accomplish? What would count as discovering I was wrong? The language-game provides no purchase for these questions.

19. My having two hands is, in normal circumstances, as certain as anything I could produce in evidence for it. That is why I am not able to take the sight of my hand as evidence for it. Evidence must be more certain than what it supports. If p is evidence for q, then certainty(p) > certainty(q). But nothing exceeds certainty of "Here is a hand" in relevant contexts. Therefore nothing can serve as evidence for it. The proposition sits at justification's terminus, not within chains of justification.

20. So one might grant Moore was right, if he is interpreted like this: a proposition saying here is a physical object may have the same logical status as one saying here is a red patch.

21. If someone were to contest my statement "That is red," I might examine the object more closely—but not to discover whether I was right or whether the object was red. I would examine it to find what it actually is. Two examination-purposes exist: (a) To verify whether the object has property F—this presupposes doubt is coherent. (b) To discover what the object is—this treats my initial statement as misidentification, not as potentially false while the identification holds. The latter does not grant sense to "I might be wrong that it's red while it is red."

22. The same thing applies here as applies to the proposition "I know that that's a..."

22. The claim "I know that that's a . . ." and the claim "I know that there's a hand here" exhibit parallel logical features. Consider what follows from these utterances and what does not. From the proposition "There is a hand there," one can derive "He knows that there's a hand there"—but only if the conditions for knowledge obtain. Conversely, "There is a hand there" follows from "He knows that there's a hand there." This reciprocal entailment relation holds because knowledge entails truth. But—and this is the crucial asymmetry—from his utterance "I know . . ." it does not follow that he does know it. The utterance itself, considered merely as an utterance, does not guarantee the truth of what it purports to express.

14. That he does know takes some showing. The utterance "I know" is not self-certifying. It requires independent confirmation.

15. What is required is a demonstration that no mistake was possible in the circumstances. Merely giving the assurance "I know" doesn't suffice for this purpose. The assurance amounts to nothing more than the speaker's claim that he cannot be making a mistake. But this is precisely what stands in need of objective establishment. It needs to be objectively established that the speaker is not making a mistake about his own incapacity for error. The claim of infallibility requires vindication from outside the claim itself.

16. The formula "If I know something, then I also know that I know it, and so on" embodies a particular conception of what "I know that" means. According to this conception, "I know that" means "I am incapable of being wrong about that." The claim to knowledge is equivalent to the claim of immunity from error. But whether I am so immune is not settled by my assertion of immunity. It needs to be established objectively—that is, by reference to considerations independent of my subjective assurance.

17. Suppose I say "I'm incapable of being wrong about this: that is a book" while pointing to an object. What would constitute a mistake in such a case? And do I have any clear conception of what such a mistake would be like? To claim incapacity for error is to rule out certain possibilities. But if I cannot specify what I am ruling out, the claim lacks determinate content. The notion of a mistake here needs to be articulated in concrete terms, not merely gestured at through the assertion of its impossibility.

18. The statement "I know" often means: I have the proper grounds for my assertion. In contexts where this is what is meant, the grounds can be exhibited or described. If the other person is acquainted with the language-game in question—if he understands the practice within which the claim is made—he would be in a position to admit that I know, provided the grounds are adequate. The other person, if he is acquainted with the language-game, must be able to imagine how one may know something of the kind in question. That is, he must be able to recognize what would constitute adequate grounds for such a claim. The intelligibility of knowledge-claims depends on shared understanding of what counts as grounds.

19. The statement "I know that here is a hand" may be continued with "for it's my hand that I'm looking at." This continuation provides the grounds. In such a case, a reasonable person will not doubt that I know. The grounds are sufficient to remove reasonable doubt. Nor will the idealist doubt this, at least not in the ordinary sense of doubt. Rather, he will say that he was not concerned with the practical doubt which is being dismissed by the appeal to grounds. He will insist that there is a further doubt behind that one—a philosophical doubt that persists even when practical grounds are adequate. That this further doubt is an illusion has to be shown in a different way. It cannot be shown by adducing further grounds of the same kind, since the idealist does not question the adequacy of such grounds within their own domain. The illusion must be exposed by clarifying the logical character of the supposed doubt itself.

20. "Doubting the existence of the external world" does not mean doubting the existence of some particular object in the way one might doubt, for example, the existence of a planet which later observations proved to exist. The philosophical doubt is not resolved by the same methods that resolve empirical doubts. Or does Moore want to say that knowing that here is his hand is different in kind from knowing of the existence of the planet Saturn? If the knowledge-claims are of the same kind, then it would be possible to point out the discovery of the planet Saturn to those who doubt the external world and say that its existence has been proved, and hence the existence of the external world as well. But this is manifestly not possible. The discovery of Saturn does not refute idealism. This suggests that the two knowledge-claims differ in kind, or that the philosophical doubt is not genuinely comparable to empirical doubt.

21. Moore's view comes down to this: the concept 'know' is analogous to the concepts 'believe', 'surmise', 'doubt', 'be convinced' in that the statement "I know . . ." cannot be a mistake. According to this view, to say "I know" is to exclude the possibility of error in the same way that "I believe" or "I am convinced" report psychological states that cannot be mistaken as reports of those states. And if that is so—if "I know" is incorrigible in this way—then there can be an inference from such an utterance to the truth of the assertion made. From "I know that p" one could infer "p." But here the form "I thought I knew" is being overlooked. This form shows that "I know" is not incorrigible in the way Moore supposes. If "I thought I knew" is admissible—if it makes sense to say this—then "I know" does not guarantee truth. But if "I thought I knew" is inadmissible—if saying "I know" does preclude the possibility of mistake—then a mistake in the assertion must be logically impossible. The incorrigibility would have to be absolute. And anyone who is acquainted with the language-game must realize this. If mistakes are logically impossible, no assurance from a reliable person that he knows can contribute anything beyond the assertion itself. The assurance would be redundant, adding nothing to what is already guaranteed by the logical character of the claim.

22. It would surely be remarkable if we had to believe the reliable person who says "I can't be wrong," or who says "I am not wrong." The statement of one's own reliability or infallibility does not constitute evidence for it. If someone's mere assertion of incapacity for error were sufficient grounds for believing him incapable of error, then anyone could establish his own infallibility by declaration. But this is absurd. Reliability and incapacity for error must be established by something other than the subject's own assurance. The assurance itself stands in need of vindication.

23. If I don't know whether someone has two hands—if I don't know, say, whether they have been amputated or not—I shall believe his assurance that he has two hands, if he is trustworthy. My belief rests on his trustworthiness, established on prior grounds. And if he says he knows it, that can only signify to me that he has been able to make sure. It signifies that he has ascertained the fact by appropriate means—that his arms are not still concealed by coverings and bandages, and so forth. My believing the trustworthy person stems from my admitting that it is possible for him to make sure. I understand what it would be for him to verify the claim, and I trust that he has done so. But someone who says that perhaps there are no physical objects makes no such admission. He does not admit the possibility of verification because he questions the very framework within which verification operates. The idealist's doubt is not a doubt about whether verification has occurred; it is a doubt about whether verification is possible in principle.

24. The idealist's question would be something like: "What right have I not to doubt the existence of my hands?" This is a request for justification of the absence of doubt. And to that question, the answer cannot be: I know that they exist. For the idealist is questioning precisely whether such knowledge is possible. But someone who asks such a question is overlooking the fact that a doubt about existence only works in a language-game. Doubt has application only within a context that provides criteria for what counts as doubt and what would resolve it. Hence, we should first have to ask: what would such a doubt be like? We would need to clarify what form this doubt takes, what would count as entertaining it, what would count as resolving it. And we don't understand this straight off. The idealist's doubt does not present itself as a recognizable instance of doubt within any established practice.

25. One may be wrong even about "there being a hand here." The possibility of error is not absolutely excluded. Only in particular circumstances is it impossible to be wrong. The circumstances determine whether error is possible. "Even in a calculation one can be wrong—only in certain circumstances one can't." Calculation provides a parallel case. Under normal circumstances, with clear notation, adequate attention, and checking procedures, one cannot go wrong in simple calculations. But in other circumstances—distraction, poor notation, complexity—error remains possible.

26. But can it be seen from a rule what circumstances logically exclude a mistake in the employment of rules of calculation? Can the rule itself specify the conditions under which its application is infallible? What use is a rule to us here? A rule for applying rules threatens regress. Mightn't we go wrong in applying the second-order rule, just as we might go wrong in applying the first-order rule?

27. If one wanted to give something like a rule here, then it would contain the expression "in normal circumstances." The rule would have to invoke normality. And we recognize normal circumstances but cannot precisely describe them. We can identify instances of normality when we encounter them, but we cannot specify necessary and sufficient conditions. At most, we can describe a range of abnormal circumstances—cases of distraction, defective notation, unusual complexity, and so forth. The normal is understood by contrast with the specifiable abnormal, not through independent characterization.

28. What is 'learning a rule'?—This. What is 'making a mistake in applying it'?—This. And what is pointed to here is something indeterminate. Learning and mistake-making are shown through examples, through training, through practice. They are not defined by explicit criteria that could be stated in advance and applied mechanically.

29. Practice in the use of the rule also shows what is a mistake in its employment. Through repeated application, one comes to recognize errors as errors. The ability to identify mistakes is acquired along with the ability to follow the rule.

30. When someone has made sure of something, he says: "Yes, the calculation is right." But he did not infer that from his condition of certainty. He did not reason from the fact that he feels certain to the conclusion that the calculation is correct. One does not infer how things are from one's own certainty. Certainty is, as it were, a tone of voice in which one declares how things are. It is the manner of assertion, not a premise from which the assertion is derived. But one does not infer from the tone of voice that one is justified. The tone expresses confidence; it does not constitute justification.

31. The propositions which one comes back to again and again as if bewitched—these I should like to expunge from philosophical language. Certain propositions exert a peculiar fascination, drawing us into repetitive and fruitless patterns of thought. These need to be set aside, not refuted.

32. It's not a matter of Moore's knowing that there's a hand there. The issue is not whether Moore possesses this piece of knowledge. Rather, we should not understand him if he were to say "Of course I may be wrong about this." The assertion of possible error would be unintelligible in this context. We should ask "What is it like to make such a mistake as that?"—for example, what's it like to discover that it was a mistake? What would constitute the discovery that what one took to be one's hand was not in fact a hand? If no answer can be given, the supposed possibility of error is empty.

33. Thus we expunge the sentences that don't get us any further. We set aside utterances that generate no clarification, that lead to no resolution, that merely perplex.

34. If someone is taught to calculate, is he also taught that he can rely on a calculation of his teacher's? Is trust in the teacher's calculations explicitly inculcated, or does it form part of the background against which teaching occurs? But these explanations must after all sometime come to an end. Justification reaches a point beyond which it cannot proceed. Will the learner also be taught that he can trust his senses—since he is indeed told in many cases that in such and such a special case you cannot trust them? Rule and exception both figure in training. The general reliability of the senses is presupposed; specific unreliabilities are noted.

35. But can't it be imagined that there should be no physical objects? I don't know. The coherence of such an imagination is unclear. And yet "There are physical objects" is nonsense if construed as an empirical proposition. Is it supposed to be an empirical proposition? Empirical propositions are verified or falsified by observation. But what observation could verify or falsify this?

**35.** Consider now this proposition: "There seem to be physical objects." Is this an empirical proposition?

**36.** When we say "A is a physical object," we offer a piece of instruction. To whom is this instruction addressed? It is addressed to someone who does not yet understand what "A" designates, or what "physical object" means. The statement thus functions as instruction about word-use. "Physical object" is a logical concept—comparable to color, quantity, and similar categorizing terms. Precisely because "physical object" functions in this way, no such proposition as "There are physical objects" can be coherently formulated. Yet attempts to formulate such propositions are encountered constantly.

**37.** But does it suffice, as an answer to the idealist's skepticism or to the realist's assurances, simply to declare that "There are physical objects" is nonsense? After all, for them it is not nonsense. A more adequate response would be this: the assertion in question, together with its opposite, constitutes a misfiring attempt to express something that cannot be expressed in that manner. That the attempt misfires can be demonstrated. But demonstrating this is not the conclusion of the matter. We must recognize that what initially presents itself as the first expression of a difficulty—or of that difficulty's solution—may not yet be correctly expressed at all. Compare: someone who has a legitimate criticism of a painting will often initially direct the criticism at the wrong feature of the work. Investigation is required to identify the proper target for the critic's objection.

**38.** Consider knowledge in mathematics. Here one must continually remind oneself of the unimportance of the "inner process" or "state." One must ask: "Why should such a state be important? What does it matter to me?" What is of interest is not the inner accompaniment but rather how we use mathematical propositions.

**39.** Calculation is performed in a particular manner. Under certain circumstances, a calculation is treated as absolutely reliable—as certainly correct.

**40.** The assertion "I know that here is my hand" may be followed by the question "How do you know?" The answer to that question presupposes that this can be known in that particular way. Instead of saying "I know that here is my hand," one might therefore say "Here is my hand," and subsequently specify how one knows this.

**41.** The statement "I know where I am feeling pain" or "I know that I feel it here" is as incorrect as "I know that I am in pain." But "I know where you touched my arm" is correct.

**42.** One can say "He believes it, but it isn't so," yet one cannot say "He knows it, but it isn't so." Does this difference stem from a difference between the mental states of belief and knowledge? No. Consider what might be called a "mental state": what is expressed by tone of voice, by gestures, and similar manifestations. In this sense, one might speak of a mental state of conviction, and that state may be identical whether what obtains is knowledge or false belief. To suppose that different states must correspond to the words "believe" and "know" would be like supposing that different persons must correspond to the word "I" and to the name "Ludwig," simply because the concepts are different.

**43.** What sort of proposition is this: "We cannot have miscalculated in 12 × 12 = 144"? It must be a proposition of logic. But is it not the same as—or does it not amount to the same thing as—the statement that 12 × 12 = 144?

**44.** If one demands a rule from which it follows that there cannot have been a miscalculation here, the answer is that we did not learn this through a rule. We learned it by learning to calculate.

**45.** We came to know the nature of calculating by learning to calculate.

**46.** But cannot one describe how we satisfy ourselves concerning the reliability of a calculation? Certainly one can. Yet no rule emerges when we undertake such a description. The most important point is this: the rule is not needed. Nothing is lacking. We calculate according to a rule, and that suffices.

**47.** This is how one calculates. Calculating is this—what we learn in school, for example. One must set aside the notion of a transcendent certainty connected with one's concept of mind or spirit.

**48.** Nevertheless, from among many calculations, certain ones might be designated as reliable once and for all, while others are designated as not yet fixed. Is this a logical distinction?

**49.** But remember: even when a calculation is something fixed for me, this fixity is merely a decision made for a practical purpose.

**50.** When does one say "I know that ... × ... = ..."? One says this when one has checked the calculation.

**51.** What sort of proposition is "What could a mistake here be like?"? It would have to be a logical proposition. But it belongs to a logic that is not employed in the usual way, because what it tells us is not learned through propositions. It is a logical proposition in that it describes the conceptual—or linguistic—situation.

**52.** The conceptual situation is therefore not the same for a proposition such as "At this distance from the sun there is a planet" and for a proposition such as "Here is a hand" (namely my own hand). The second cannot be characterized as a hypothesis. But there is no sharp boundary separating these cases.

**53.** One might therefore concede that Moore was correct, provided he is interpreted as follows: a proposition stating that here is a physical object may possess the same logical status as a proposition stating that here is a red patch.

**54.** For it is false to say that error merely becomes increasingly improbable as we move from the case of the planet to the case of my own hand. No: at some point, error has ceased to be conceivable. This is already suggested by the following consideration: if matters were otherwise, it would also be conceivable that we are mistaken in every statement we make about physical objects—that any such statement we make is erroneous.

**55.** Is the hypothesis conceivable that all the things surrounding us do not exist? Would that hypothesis not resemble the hypothesis that we have miscalculated in all our calculations?

**56.** When one says "Perhaps this planet does not exist and the light-phenomenon arises in some other way," one requires an example of an object that does exist. One means: this does not exist—as, for example, this other thing does. Or are we to say that certainty is merely a constructed limiting point to which some things approximate more closely and others less closely? No. Doubt gradually loses its sense. This language-game simply is like that. Everything that describes a language-game belongs to logic.

**57.** Might not "I know—I am not merely surmising—that here is my hand" be conceived as a proposition of grammar? Hence as not temporal. But in that case, is it not analogous to this proposition: "I know—I am not merely surmising—that I am seeing red"?

**Explanation of the grammatical character.** If "I know that here is my hand" functions as a grammatical proposition, then it states a rule for the use of expressions within a language-game. It would not report a temporal state—something that obtains now but might not obtain later. The analogy to "I know that I am seeing red" tests this interpretation. In that case too one would have a proposition that purports to express knowledge of what is immediately before one. But does "I know I am seeing red" function grammatically, as a rule about color-predication? Or does it remain a temporal assertion, one that could in principle be false?

**Problem with the analogy.** The situations differ in this respect: "Here is my hand" admits of the question "How do you know?" in a way that "I am seeing red" does not. The epistemic route to the hand-proposition may be specified—by sight, by touch. But "I am seeing red" already specifies the epistemic route. To add "I know" contributes nothing; the assertion "I am seeing red" already excludes doubt. Thus the grammatical interpretation fits the color-case less readily than the hand-case.

**Alternative consideration.** If both are grammatical propositions, they articulate the bounds of the language-game in which they occur. "Here is my hand" would then mark a boundary beyond which doubt becomes senseless—not false, but senseless. Similarly, "I am seeing red" would mark the point at which asking for justification loses application. The propositions would not describe psychological states of certainty but rather logical limits.

**Return to the mathematical case.** The reliability of a calculation exhibits a parallel structure. "We cannot have miscalculated in 12 × 12 = 144" does not express heightened subjective confidence. It articulates the role this calculation plays within our mathematical practice. The proposition is logical in character because it describes how we treat certain calculations—as beyond revision.

**But then, what of practical decisions?** It was noted that even when a calculation is fixed for me, this is a decision for practical purposes. Does this undermine the claim that such fixity is logical rather than empirical? No. The decision concerns which propositions will serve as hinges within a particular activity. That some propositions must serve this function is a logical point. Which propositions so serve may be determined practically.

**The concept of a mistake.** "What could a mistake here be like?" asks for the conceptual space within which error is intelligible. Where no such space can be delineated, talk of mistake loses application. This does not mean mistakes are improbable; it means the concept "mistake" has reached its limit. The proposition "Here is a hand" (my own hand) occupies such a position. Not because hands are epistemically privileged objects, but because this proposition functions within our language-game as a standard against which other claims are measured.

**Conclusion regarding Moore.** If Moore's assertion is interpreted as claiming that "Here is a physical object" and "Here is a red patch" share logical status—both serving as uncheckable standards within their respective contexts—then the interpretation succeeds. But if Moore intended an empirical claim about comparative certainty, the interpretation fails. The question is not whether we are more certain of hands than planets, but whether the role these propositions play admits of doubt at all.

The proposition "So there are physical objects" resembles the proposition "So there are colors." Both purport to be conclusions drawn from certain premises, but the logical character of such conclusions requires examination.

58. Suppose we construe "I know etc." as a grammatical proposition. Given this construal, the "I" becomes inessential. The proposition signifies that doubt has no application in the relevant case—that is, the expression "I do not know" fails to make sense in this context. And given that "I do not know" lacks sense here, it follows that "I know" likewise lacks sense in this context. The affirmation and its negation both fail to function as they do in contexts where doubt is intelligible.

This consequence merits attention. Propositions that admit genuine knowledge-claims permit corresponding expressions of ignorance or uncertainty. When I say "I know that water boils at 100°C at sea level," the claim makes sense precisely because "I do not know at what temperature water boils" also makes sense. Someone might lack this information; the temperature might be discovered through investigation; alternative hypotheses might be entertained and rejected. But when we consider propositions like "I know I have hands" or "I know the earth has existed for many years," the corresponding expressions of doubt—"I do not know whether I have hands," "I do not know whether the earth has existed for many years"—fail to occupy any intelligible position in our linguistic practices. They do not express a mere lack of information that investigation might remedy. They indicate something has gone wrong with the speaker's grasp of the concepts involved.

59. The claim "I know" functions here as a logical insight rather than an empirical discovery. It articulates something about the role certain propositions play in our thought and language. But this logical character means that "I know" cannot serve as a proof of realism. Realism—the philosophical thesis that physical objects exist independently of our perceptions or beliefs about them—is not established by pointing to propositions that function as hinges in our system of beliefs. The logical insight concerns how certain propositions function, not what metaphysical conclusions follow from their functioning in that way.

60. Consider the claim that this is a piece of paper. It would be wrong to say that this constitutes a hypothesis subject to empirical confirmation or disconfirmation through subsequent experience. When I say "I know that this is a piece of paper," the "I know" relates neither to an empirical hypothesis of the sort scientific investigation tests, nor to a logical determination of the sort deductive reasoning establishes. 

The error lies in assimilating this case to cases where hypotheses genuinely operate. A scientific hypothesis—say, that a particular chemical compound will precipitate under certain conditions—is confirmed when predicted observations occur and disconfirmed when they fail to occur. The hypothesis occupies a position in a structure of reasoning where alternatives compete, where evidence accumulates, where revision remains possible. But "This is a piece of paper" (when said while holding a piece of paper in plain view) does not function as a hypothesis in this sense. It does not compete with alternative hypotheses; additional evidence does not accumulate in its favor; the question of its revision does not arise through ordinary empirical means.

61. A word's meaning is a kind of employment of that word. What we learn when a word becomes incorporated into our language is how to employ it—in what contexts it occurs, what follows from its application, what contrasts govern its use, what role it plays in various language-games. Meaning is not an invisible entity the word names, nor a mental image the word evokes, but a pattern of employment in linguistic practice.

62. This understanding explains the correspondence between the concepts "rule" and "meaning." To grasp a word's meaning is to grasp the rules governing its employment. These rules need not be explicit—indeed, they typically are not formulated as explicit principles. But they operate nonetheless. We manifest our grasp of them through our applications of the word, our corrections of misapplications, our explanations to those learning the language. The rules governing a word's use constitute what we might call its meaning.

63. Imagine facts otherwise than they are. Suppose humans lacked hands, or material objects regularly vanished and reappeared without cause, or the past continually rearranged itself. In such circumstances, certain language-games would lose their importance while others would gain importance. This would produce an alteration—gradual rather than sudden—in how we employ the vocabulary of our language. Words currently central to our practices might recede to marginal status; words currently peripheral might become fundamental; new words might enter to address concerns our current vocabulary cannot accommodate.

64. A word's meaning may be compared with an official's function. Just as different officials perform different functions within an institution, different words perform different functions within language. And just as a single term might designate officials who perform different functions in different contexts (consider "secretary" in different organizational structures), a single word might have different meanings—that is, different patterns of employment—in different language-games.

65. When language-games change, concepts change. And when concepts change, the meanings of words change. This succession is not accidental. Language-games constitute the contexts within which words acquire meaning through use. Alter the language-game and you alter the context of employment. Alter the context of employment and you alter meaning itself.

66. I make assertions about reality, and these assertions carry different degrees of assurance. How does this difference in degree manifest itself? What consequences follow from it? 

We might be dealing with the certainty of memory, or with the certainty of perception. I may be certain of something while remaining cognizant of what sort of test might convince me of error. I am quite sure, for example, of the date of a particular battle. But should I discover a different date in a recognized historical work, I would alter my opinion. This alteration would not mean I had lost all confidence in my capacity for judgment. It would mean I had encountered a specific reason for revising a specific belief.

67. Could we imagine a person who continually makes mistakes where we regard mistakes as impossible? He states that he lives at such and such a place, has reached such and such an age, comes from such and such a city. He speaks with the same certainty—provides all the conventional tokens of certainty—that I do when making parallel statements about myself. But he is wrong. What is his relation to this error? What should we suppose about him? The question concerns what the logician should say about this case.

69. I am inclined to say: "If I am wrong about this, I have no guarantee that anything I say is true." But others will not say this about me, nor will I say it about other people. The asymmetry is significant. Certain errors would undermine not merely particular beliefs but the entire framework within which belief and error operate. Yet we do not attribute such framework-undermining errors to one another in ordinary circumstances.

70. For months I have lived at address A. I have read the street name and house number countless times, received countless letters at this address, given countless people this address. If I am wrong about it, the error is scarcely less significant than if I were to believe (wrongly) that I was writing Chinese rather than German. Both errors would indicate not a mere factual mistake but a profound disturbance in my relation to my circumstances.

71. If my friend were to imagine one day that he had been living for a long time at some place other than where he has actually been living, I would not call this a mistake. I would call it a mental disturbance, perhaps a transient one. The distinction matters.

72. Not every false belief of this kind qualifies as a mistake.

73. But what distinguishes mistake from mental disturbance? Or rather, what distinguishes my treating something as a mistake from my treating it as mental disturbance? The difference lies not solely in the belief's content but in how it relates to the person's other beliefs and to the framework within which belief operates.

74. Can we say that a mistake has not only a cause but also a ground? That is, roughly: when someone makes a mistake, this mistake can be fitted into what the person knows correctly. The false belief occupies a position within a larger structure of true beliefs. It represents a local failure rather than a systematic breakdown.

75. Consider two cases. First: I wrongly believe that there is a table here in front of me (perhaps sophisticated optical equipment has produced a convincing illusion). This might still be a mistake—a local error compatible with my cognitive functioning remaining intact. Second: I wrongly believe that I have seen this table, or one like it, every day for several months, and have regularly used it. This is not a mistake in the same sense. The second case involves not a perceptual error but a massive distortion of my relation to my past and my environment.

76. My aim must be to articulate what the statements one would like to make here amount to, even though these statements cannot be made in ways that carry standard significance. The difficulty lies in expressing insights that concern the conditions for significance itself.

77. Perhaps I perform a multiplication twice to ensure accuracy, or perhaps I ask someone else to check the calculation. But shall I perform it twenty times, or ask twenty people to verify it? And would declining to do so constitute negligence? Would the certainty really be greater for being checked twenty times? And can I give a reason why it would not be greater? At some point, continued checking ceases to increase certainty and instead indicates doubt about whether we understand what we are doing.

79. That I am a man and not a woman can be verified. But if I were to say I was a woman, and then tried to explain the error by noting that I had not checked the statement, the explanation would not be accepted. The purported explanation reveals that something has gone wrong not with my information but with my grasp of the concepts involved or my relation to elementary facts about myself.

80. The truth of my statements is the test of my understanding of these statements. This formulation inverts our ordinary thinking. We typically suppose that understanding precedes judgment: first we understand a statement, then we determine whether it is true. But understanding manifests itself through patterns of judgment. Systematic falsehood in a domain indicates incomprehension.

81. That is to say: if I make certain false statements, it becomes uncertain whether I understand them. A person who regularly applies "red" to green objects, "square" to circular objects, "Tuesday" to Thursday, exhibits not merely false beliefs but failure to grasp the concepts involved. Truth and understanding are internally related.

82. What counts as an adequate test of a statement belongs to logic. It belongs to the description of the language-game. Different statements admit different tests. What verifies "Water is boiling" differs from what verifies "The Battle of Hastings occurred in 1066," which differs from what verifies "This argument is valid." These differences are not arbitrary but constitutive of what the statements mean.

83. The truth of certain empirical propositions belongs to our frame of reference. These propositions do not stand within the frame, subject to testing against it, but constitute the frame itself.

84. Moore says he knows that the earth existed long before his birth. Expressed thus, the claim seems to concern Moore personally—what this particular man knows. It simultaneously seems to be a statement about the physical world. Now whether Moore knows this or that particular fact is philosophically uninteresting. But it is interesting that, and how, such things can be known.

Had Moore informed us that he knew the distance separating certain stars, we might conclude that he had conducted specialized investigations, and we would want to know what these were. Astronomical distances are not common knowledge; determining them requires particular expertise and methods. But Moore chooses precisely a case in which all of us seem to know the same thing he knows, and without being able to say how we know it.

I believe, for example, that I know as much about this matter (the earth's existence before my birth) as Moore knows. And if he knows it as he says, then I know it too. For he has not arrived at his proposition through special investigation unavailable to others.
