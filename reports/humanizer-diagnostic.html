The Legality-Morality Distinction and the Limits of Legal Obligation

Given only that an action falls within the bounds of what statute permits, how does it follow that one ought to perform that action? How does it follow that the specific justifications we offer for our conduct are ever at all principled?

We have seen that the integrity of a moral framework depends on the integrity of the normative connections it hosts. To the extent that actions are morally independent of each other—governed solely by statute rather than by shared ethical commitments—there is no sense in which they constitute some unified normative order.

The attempt to collapse morality into legality presupposes that moral principles can simply be suspended or ignored. That presupposition in its turn assumes that moral principles are separable from the social fabric; it assumes, in other words, that they are not constitutive of that fabric.

That assumption is erroneous. Suppose that one or more moral principles governing a society were eliminated or replaced. First of all, it wouldn't be possible to eliminate just one such principle. Given a society in which promise-keeping carried no moral weight, or in which fiduciary obligations were unrecognized, or in which any single one of the principles operative in functional communities were replaced by some other principle, a number of other principles that sustain social cooperation would also have to be abandoned.

Moral principles are not independent of one another. In actuality, trust is directly proportional to reliability. If it were inversely proportional to reliability, every one of the norms governing interpersonal relations would have to be jettisoned or heavily modified.

Consider a museum curator responsible for priceless artifacts. Legally, she might auction them to the highest bidder and pocket the proceeds, provided she navigates bankruptcy law cleverly enough to shield the transaction. Morally, she holds those objects in trust for the public; her legal authority is instrumental, not absolute. The distinction here resembles that between owning a rescue dog and owning a lamp. Both are your legal property. But if you smash the lamp, you've destroyed something you owned; if you starve the dog, you've betrayed a dependent creature despite your formal ownership. The law recognizes your dominion in both cases, yet morality draws a boundary the law may not. Legal permission is the outer fence; moral obligation is the path within it.

Different principles, different social fabric. Different social fabric, different community.
