--- Level 3 (518 statements) ---

1.0 Some knowledge is direct, and some knowledge is indirect.
1.1 Indirect knowledge is acquired through inference.
1.1.1 An inference forms a new belief on the basis of an old belief.
1.1.2 A legitimate inference conforms to a legitimate rule of inference.
1.1.3 A legitimate rule of inference corresponds to a dependence-relation.
1.2 Dependence-relations are either logical or causal.
1.2.1 Logical dependence holds between propositions.
1.2.2 Causal dependence holds between states of affairs.
1.2.3 A proposition is either true or false.
1.2.4 A state of affairs occupies space-time.
1.3 A triangle depends logically on having three sides.
1.3.1 Smith’s passing depends causally on Smith’s studying.
1.3.2 Logical dependence is interpropositional.
1.3.3 Causal dependence is objectual.
2.0 Knowledge concerns propositions rather than objects.
2.1 A belief has a proposition as its object.
2.1.1 Whatever is known is believed.
2.1.2 Whatever is believed is a proposition.
2.1.3 Rocks, trees, and people may be objects of awareness without being objects of knowledge.
2.1.4 One knows truths about objects rather than objects themselves.
2.2 Knowledge is conceptually articulated awareness.
2.2.1 Awareness may be propositional or objectual.
2.2.2 Objectual awareness does not require possession of the concepts that articulate it.
2.2.3 Propositional awareness requires conceptual articulation.
2.3 Explanation concerns truths rather than objects.
2.3.1 An observation is an objectual awareness.
2.3.2 An observation must be converted into a proposition to be explained.
2.3.3 Explaining an avalanche is explaining why it is true that the avalanche occurred.
3.0 Analytic knowledge concerns logical dependence-relations.
3.1 Analytic truths are known through conceptual analysis rather than sensory observation.
3.1.1 Analytic knowledge may be direct or inferential.
3.1.2 The truth that \(P\) implies \(P\) or \(Q\) expresses a logical dependence.
3.1.3 Arithmetic truths express dependence-relations among sets or numerical structures.
3.1.4 Conceptual truths may concern mathematics, language, sentience, government, or definitions.
3.2 Causal knowledge is empirical.
3.2.1 Causal relations are known through the senses or through real-time awareness of one’s own psychological processes.
3.2.2 Causal necessity differs from logical necessity.
3.2.3 A triangle’s having three sides is logically necessary.
3.2.4 An ice-cube’s melting on a hot surface is causally necessary.
3.3 Theoretical knowledge is inferential.
3.3.1 Not every inferential belief is theoretical knowledge.
3.3.2 A theory is a proposition describing a relatively comprehensive fact about the structure of the universe.
3.3.3 Knowledge of a theory is theoretical knowledge.
3.3.4 Knowledge derived from a theory is also theoretical knowledge.
4.0 Knowledge is justified true belief acquired through the appropriate connection with reality.
4.1 Knowledge entails belief, truth, and legitimate justification.
4.1.1 Truth and justification alone do not guarantee knowledge.
4.1.2 A broken clock may yield a true belief without producing knowledge.
4.1.3 A belief is knowledge only if it was acquired through the relevant reality.
4.2 The relevant connection is information-transmissive.
4.2.1 An information-transmissive structure preserves the structure of its source.
4.2.2 A merely evidence-generative relation need not preserve structure.
4.2.3 The difference between knowledge and belief is not statistical.
4.2.4 A belief is knowledge exactly when it terminates an information-transmissive series.
4.3 Reliability is not sufficient for knowledge.
4.3.1 An unreliable mechanism may yield knowledge in a context where it transmits information.
4.3.2 A reliable mechanism may fail to yield knowledge in a context where it does not transmit information.
4.3.3 An honest witness does not provide knowledge unless the testimony transmits information.
4.3.4 A dishonest witness may provide knowledge if the testimony transmits information.
5.0 Information is structure-preserving causal transmission.
5.1 A causal series consists of events in which each event causes the next.
5.1.1 A structure-preserving series transmits the structure of its initial event to its terminal event.
5.1.2 An information-bearing series is a structure-preserving series.
5.1.3 Information-transmission is structure-preservation.
5.2 All information is evidence, but not all evidence is information.
5.2.1 Evidence exists when one state of affairs gives reason to believe in another through a causal relation.
5.2.2 Evidence need not preserve the structure of what it indicates.
5.2.3 An elevator’s stopping may evidence someone’s pressing a button without transmitting the button-press’s structure.
5.3 Perception depends on information-transmission.
5.3.1 Sense-perceptions are structurally related to the objects they represent.
5.3.2 A perception is veridical when it inherits its structure from its object.
5.3.3 A perception is accurate to the extent that it preserves the object’s structure.
5.3.4 Perception-based beliefs are accurate to the extent that their structures are inherited from external realities.
5.4 Knowledge based on testimony requires an information-transmissive link to the relevant reality.
5.4.1 Knowledge of evidential relations presupposes knowledge acquired through information-transmission.
6.0 Continuity is the basis of causation and information-transmission.
6.1 An effect is a continuation of its cause.
6.1.1 An event transmits information about its cause to the extent that it inherits the cause’s structure.
6.1.2 Causal information-transmission preserves the mutual relations among concurrent causal lines.
6.1.3 Structure-preserving causation allows the cause to be inferred from the effect without ad hoc knowledge.
6.2 Temporal precedence is defined causally.
6.2.1 One event precedes another when a possible causal process begins with the first and ends with the second.
6.2.2 Two events are simultaneous when no such causal process connects them.
6.3 Causal continuity underlies knowledge.
6.3.1 Thoughts inherit their structures from perceptions.
6.3.2 Perceptions inherit their structures from external realities.
6.3.3 Knowledge of non-information-transmissive evidence depends on prior information-transmissive knowledge.
7.0 Deduction is inference from entailment, whereas induction is inference from confirmation.
7.1 \(P\) entails \(Q\) when \(Q\) is a logical consequence of \(P\).
7.1.1 If \(P\) is true and entails \(Q\), then \(Q\) is true.
7.1.2 Inferring \(Q\) from \(P\) on the basis of their entailment is deductive inference.
7.2 \(P\) confirms \(Q\) when \(P\) increases the likelihood of \(Q\).
7.2.1 Confirmation does not by itself license inference.
7.2.2 \(P\) may confirm both \(Q\) and a proposition incompatible with \(Q\).
7.2.3 \(P\) may increase the probability of \(Q\) without making \(Q\) probable.
7.2.4 A further truth may combine with \(P\) to disconfirm \(Q\).
7.3 Inductive inference requires explanatory superiority.
7.3.1 \(Q\) may be inferred from \(P\) when \(P\) and not-\(Q\) have fewer unexplained consequences than \(P\) and \(Q\).
7.3.2 \(Q\) may be inferred from \(P\) when \(P\) has fewer unexplained consequences than not-\(P\).
7.3.3 Knowledge of these conditions makes the inference legitimate.
7.4 Entailment is transitive and monotonic.
7.4.1 If \(P\) entails \(Q\), and \(Q\) entails \(R\), then \(P\) entails \(R\).
7.4.2 If \(P\) entails \(Q\), adding \(R\) does not defeat the entailment.
7.5 Confirmation is non-transitive and non-monotonic.
7.5.1 \(P\)’s confirming \(Q\), together with \(Q\)’s confirming \(R\), does not imply that \(P\) confirms \(R\).
7.5.2 \(P\)’s confirming \(Q\), together with \(R\)’s truth, does not imply that \(P\) and \(R\) confirm \(Q\).
8.0 Natural laws are conditional principles with broad explanatory scope.
8.1 A conditional has the form “if \(P\), then \(Q\).”
8.1.1 Expressions such as “given that” and “assuming that” also introduce conditionals.
8.1.2 Entailment is logical, whereas confirmation is explanatory.
8.1.3 A proposition is counterlogical when its structure prevents its truth.
8.1.4 A proposition is counterexplanatory when its truth has more unexplained consequences than its falsity.
8.2 Laws are commonly universal generalizations.
8.2.1 A law such as “copper expands when heated” concerns a class of conditionals.
8.2.2 A law applies to any object satisfying its antecedent.
8.2.3 A law supports predictions, counterfactuals, and explanations.
8.3 Natural laws may concern merely possible objects.
8.3.1 Some laws concern classes with no actual members.
8.3.2 Some laws concern limiting cases prohibited by the laws themselves.
8.3.3 Actual behavior is understood through approximation to such limiting cases.
8.4 Scientific knowledge of imperceptibles is conditional knowledge.
8.4.1 Knowledge of the future is conditional.
8.4.2 Knowledge of the past is conditional.
8.4.3 Knowledge of the non-actual possible is conditional.
8.4.4 Knowledge of the present imperceptible is conditional.
8.5 Imperceptibility may arise from severed causal access, limited scale, or natural impossibility.
8.5.1 An object may be imperceptible because an information-bearing process cannot reach the observer.
8.5.2 An object may be imperceptible because it is too small for available technology.
8.5.3 An object may be imperceptible because natural law prevents its perception.
8.6 Laws are more important to science than singular facts.
8.6.1 A singular fact matters scientifically insofar as it instantiates a general principle.
8.6.2 A law may be place- and time-sensitive.
8.6.3 A law need not be perfectly universal to support principled inference.
8.6.4 Limited and defeasible generalizations may function as laws while knowledge remains incomplete.
8.7 Meaningful generalizations have a systemic basis.
8.7.1 A law is not merely a perfect statistical regularity.
8.7.2 A law is grounded in the system constituting the relevant properties.
8.7.3 A statistically normal occurrence may be a singularity if it is not systemically grounded.
8.7.4 A law makes its antecedent dispositive of its consequent through relatively non-context-specific structure.
9.0 Causation is a relation among events rather than objects.
9.1 Objects have causal properties but are not themselves causes or effects.
9.1.1 A rock’s weight gives it causal properties.
9.1.2 Events involving the rock, rather than the rock itself, are causes or effects.

9.2 Conditions are stable patterns of events.

9.2.3 Constant temperature consists in stable patterns of molecular change.

9.3 Ordinary changes are often meta-changes.
9.3.1 Stable constitutive changes are usually ignored.
9.3.2 A reported change commonly means a change in an underlying pattern of change.
9.4 Explanation, prediction, and control depend on causal knowledge.
9.4.1 Scientific knowledge explains past events.
9.4.2 Scientific knowledge predicts future events.
9.4.3 Scientific knowledge enables limited control over events.
9.5 Stable event-patterns make explanation possible.
9.5.1 Rogue events cannot be explained or predicted reliably.
9.5.2 Stable structures and conditions support explanation and prediction.
9.5.3 Explanation commonly identifies a program-cause rather than an immediate cause.
9.5.4 A program-cause predisposes a system toward an immediate event.
10.0 Psychological causation often involves thresholds and overdetermination.
10.1 Mental illness may have several causally distinct contributors.
10.1.1 A precondition describes the subject’s state before illness.
10.1.2 A specific cause makes a slight disturbance sufficient for illness.

10.2.1 Each contributing psychological event may justify the final event’s occurrence.
10.2.2 No single contributing event need be sufficient by itself.
10.2.3 The final event occurs when enough reasons accumulate.
10.3 Threshold effects distinguish discontinuous from continuous change.
10.3.1 A threshold mechanism remains inactive until a critical condition is reached.
10.3.2 Discontinuous change has a digital structure.
10.3.3 Continuous change has an analogue structure.
10.3.4 Causes may be insufficient individually but non-redundant within a sufficient conjunction.

11.0 Counterfactuals express logical or causal dependence.
11.1 A causal counterfactual states that one event would not have occurred without another.
11.1.1 “If Kennedy had not been assassinated, troop withdrawal would have occurred earlier” expresses causal dependence.
11.1.2 “If Smith had not fallen, his leg would not have broken” expresses causal dependence.
11.2 A logical counterfactual expresses entailment.
11.2.1 “If Smith had more than ten dollars, he would have more than five dollars” expresses logical dependence.
11.2.2 Every counterfactual affirms some form of necessitation.
11.3 Lewis’s analysis of counterfactuals is rejected.
11.3.1 Lewis evaluates counterfactuals by comparison with similar possible worlds.
11.3.2 An event has deep causal roots extending through many prior conditions.
11.3.3 Removing such an event may alter the world too radically for the proposed similarity relation to hold.
11.3.4 Counterfactual truth depends on the causal and logical structure of the actual world.
11.4 Program-causality permits causal redundancy.

12.6 The Regularity Analysis fails because temporal order presupposes causal order.

13.0 Scientific explanation cannot be reduced to the deductive-nomological model.
13.1 DN explains an event by deriving it from circumstances and laws.
13.1.1 The circumstances describe the conditions in which the event occurred.
13.1.2 The laws describe the principles operative in those circumstances.
13.1.3 The event is explained when it is a logical consequence of both.
13.2 DN presupposes causal relevance.
13.2.1 Not every prior condition is relevant to an event.
13.2.2 A condition is relevant only insofar as it caused the event.
13.2.3 Identifying relevant conditions already identifies causal information.
13.2.4 DN therefore requires an explanation before it can construct an explanation.
13.3 The distinction between nomic and accidental generalizations is causal.
13.3.1 “Metal expands when heated” is nomic because heating causes expansion.
13.3.2 “Every coin in my pocket is a quarter” is accidental because pocket-location does not cause quarter-status.

13.4 DN becomes circular when laws are defined by counterfactual support.

13.4.2 A law supports a counterfactual because its antecedent causes its consequent.

13.5 Causal knowledge is differential knowledge.

13.7 Laws do not consist in perfect regularities.

13.8 The psychological failure of DN is especially clear.

13.9.1 DN may describe scientific explanation when both cause and mechanism are known.
13.9.2 DN does not describe all explanations.
13.9.3 DN cannot identify causal relevance without presupposing causal knowledge.
14.0 The Raven Paradox results from confusing extensional coincidence with intensional law.
14.1 Logical equivalence should entail confirmational equivalence.
14.1.1 Two propositions are logically equivalent when each entails the other.
14.1.2 Two propositions are confirmationally equivalent when every datum confirms them equally.
14.1.3 Logical equivalence entails confirmational equivalence.
14.2 “All ravens are black” and “All non-black things are non-ravens” appear to generate a paradox.
14.2.1 A pink piano confirms the second formulation but not the first in ordinary reasoning.
14.2.2 The same structure appears in “heated metal expands” and “heated non-expanders are not metal.”
14.2.3 A shrinking cube steak appears to confirm only the second formulation.
14.3 Hempel’s solution fails.
14.3.1 Hempel attributes the difference to background beliefs.
14.3.2 Hempel therefore treats a pink piano as equally confirmatory of both raven statements.
14.3.3 Hempel’s solution implies that studying pianos can teach us about ravens.
14.3.4 Hempel’s solution is therefore false.
14.4 The paradox disappears when generalizations are read intensionally or extensionally.
14.4.1 An intensional generalization states that a law makes all \(\phi\)s \(\psi\)s.
14.4.2 An extensional generalization states that all \(\phi\)s happen to be \(\psi\)s.
14.4.3 “Heated metal expands” is naturally intensional.
14.4.4 Its contraposed formulation is not equivalent when read as a causal law.
14.5 The Raven Paradox is a product of the Regularity Analysis.
14.5.1 If laws were mere extensional regularities, irrelevant observations would confirm them.
14.5.2 Irrelevant observations do not confirm causal laws.
14.5.3 The paradox arises from denying causal structure.
15.0 Enumerative induction is not a legitimate form of inference.
15.1 Enumerative induction infers that unknown \(\phi\)s are \(\psi\)s from known \(\phi\)s being \(\psi\)s.
15.1.1 The observed regularity may be accidental.
15.1.2 Accidental repetition does not justify projection to unknown cases.
15.1.3 The inference becomes legitimate only when supported by an explanatory hypothesis.
15.2 Inference to the Best Explanation differs from enumerative induction.
15.2.1 IBE posits an unknown entity, structure, or mechanism.
15.2.2 The hypothesis is proposed because it removes anomalies in the available data.
15.2.3 A hypothesis explains a datum when it makes the datum continuous with the rest of reality.
15.3 Apparent enumerative induction is either IBE or no inference.
15.3.1 A person may project past success because he believes in a causal mechanism.
15.3.2 A gambler’s belief in luck is an IBE about a mechanism called Lady Luck.
15.3.3 Pavlovian association produces expectation without judgment or inference.
15.3.4 A person who explicitly denies causal connection has no rational basis for projection.
15.4 Repeated outcomes support prediction only through causal structure.
15.4.1 Repeated sixes rationally support another six when a mechanism disposes the die toward sixes.
15.4.2 Repeated sixes do not support another six when they are treated as pure coincidence.
15.4.3 The apparent induction is therefore either explanatory or irrational.
16.0 Explanation consists in eliminating discontinuities.
16.1 An IBE eliminates anomalies in the available data.
16.1.1 False hypotheses may fit limited data.
16.1.2 False hypotheses tend not to fit expanded data.
16.1.3 Additional data exposes hypotheses that cannot preserve continuity.
16.2 To explain an event is to restore its continuity with the prior universe.
16.2.1 An event is anomalous when it has no causal roots in prior conditions.
16.2.2 A cause is an antecedent that makes the effect continuous with the prior state of the universe.
16.2.3 A ball emerging from an opaque tunnel is explained by positing its continuous motion through the tunnel.
16.2.4 Explanation identifies a process of which the observed events are fragments.
16.3 IBE is justified by the structure of space-time.
16.3.1 Two events belong to one manifold only if they are causally connected.
16.3.2 A completely discontinuous post-event universe would not be a continuation of the earlier universe.
16.3.3 Memory-like contents without causal roots would not be memories.
16.3.4 Unobserved continuities must be posited when observed events belong to one manifold but their connection is unseen.
16.4 The uniformity of nature is not justified by enumerative induction.
16.4.1 The laws of physics constitute the space-time manifold.
16.4.2 A change in the laws would destroy the continuity of objects, properties, and events.
16.4.3 A universe with different laws would be a different universe.
16.4.4 The persistence of the universe presupposes the persistence of its laws.
16.5 The Uniformity Principle is a precondition of inductive reasoning.
16.5.1 If the laws changed, the objects making inferences would not persist.
16.5.2 If the objects making inferences persist, the operative laws remain uniform.
16.5.3 Inductive inference is legitimate when it fails through incompetence rather than through a defect in induction itself.
16.6 Objects are causal series.
16.6.1 Rocks, trees, stars, and natural kinds are stable event-patterns.
16.6.2 Objects exist through the forces and laws governing their parts.
16.6.3 A change in laws would terminate the objects constituted by those laws.
16.6.4 No pre-change event could cause a post-change event if the governing laws had ceased.
17.0 Determinism is law-governed temporal determination.
17.1 Determinism states that the world’s state at one time fixes its state at later times.
17.1.1 Indeterminism denies that the earlier state uniquely determines the later state.
17.1.2 The world is deterministic to the extent that laws determine later states.
17.1.3 Determinism does not entail complete predictability.
17.2 Interference limits predictability.
17.2.1 Learning about a system requires causal interaction with it.
17.2.2 Causal interaction changes the system being studied.
17.2.3 Questions may alter a person’s mental state.
17.2.4 Entering a cave and illuminating it may alter the bats being observed.
17.2.5 Complete prediction would require knowledge that does not interfere with its object.
17.3 Sensory knowledge prevents complete knowledge of initial conditions.
17.3.1 Perception requires an information-bearing causal process.
17.3.2 Learning about microphysical objects requires interactions that alter them.
17.3.3 No observer can acquire complete knowledge of the world’s microstructure.
17.3.4 Prediction requires knowledge of initial conditions and laws.
17.3.5 Complete prediction is therefore impossible.
17.4 No creature can predict exactly how its knowledge will grow.
17.4.1 If a creature knows now what it will learn later, it already knows it now.
17.4.2 What a creature learns affects what it does.
17.4.3 What it does affects the world.
17.4.4 No creature can therefore predict exactly what it will do or how the world will change.
18.0 Supervenience is dependence without identity.
18.1 One class of facts supervenes on another when change in the first requires change in the second.
18.1.1 Biological differences require relevant microphysical differences.
18.1.2 Physical identity does not entail identity of higher-order properties.
18.2 Higher-order properties may be multiply realizable.
18.2.1 Physically different objects may share aesthetic properties.
18.2.2 Physically different systems may share biological properties.
18.2.3 Hardware-distinct systems may be software-identical.
18.2.4 Hardware identity does not permit software difference.
18.3 Psychological supervenience differs from biological supervenience.
18.3.1 Physiological identity may fix psychological identity as a matter of law.
18.3.2 Psychological difference under physiological identity is counternomic rather than conceptually incoherent.
19.0 Physical law does not exhaust higher-order explanation.
19.1 Physical constraint is not physical explanation.
19.1.1 Nothing violates physical law.
19.1.2 It does not follow that every higher-order fact is explained by physical law.
19.2 A statue is not identical with the clay constituting it.
19.2.1 Constitution is distinct from identity.
19.2.2 The statue and the clay have different modal properties.
19.3 A computer is not identical with its atoms.
19.3.1 Physical laws describe the behavior of the atoms.
19.3.2 Physical laws do not thereby describe computation or storage.
19.3.3 Software concepts are invisible to physics as such.
19.3.4 Software-identical systems may have physically different realizations.
19.4 Program-determinism differs from implementation-determinism.
19.4.1 A deterministic program assigns at most one output to each relevant input.
19.4.2 Machine error is failure to implement the program.
19.4.3 Program determinism can persist despite physical implementation errors.
19.4.4 Higher-order laws may remain deterministic despite lower-order indeterminism.
19.5 Statistical trends reflect mechanisms without constituting probabilities.
19.5.1 Microphysical deviations may cancel at higher levels.
19.5.2 A higher-order system may behave deterministically despite indeterministic components.
19.5.3 Coincidental regularity may conceal the underlying probability.
19.5.4 Statistics are evidence of probabilities rather than probabilities themselves.
19.5.5 Causal mechanisms determine actual probabilities even when statistics misrepresent them.
19.6 Probability is causal possibility.
19.6.1 A malfunction has positive probability when the laws and construction permit it.
19.6.2 A behavior may be statistically certain without being causally necessary.
19.6.3 A behavior may be causally probable despite never occurring in the observed statistics.
20.0 Measurement is comparison with a standard rather than enumeration.
20.1 Length has no minimal units.
20.1.1 Between any two lengths there are intermediate lengths.
20.1.2 No object’s length can be determined merely by counting constituent units.
20.1.3 Enumeration presupposes knowledge that length is additive.
20.1.4 Length is not generally additive.
20.2 Measurement relativizes a magnitude to a standard.
20.2.1 “Longer than” gives a context-relative comparison.
20.2.2 “Two meters long” compares an object with a universal standard.
20.2.3 Every measurement is comparison with respect to some standard.
20.3 Absolute length is standardized relative length.
20.3.1 A standard is stipulated to have one unit of length.
20.3.2 An object’s measured length is its relation to that standard.
20.3.3 Rational ratios may be determined by finite subdivision.
20.3.4 Irrational ratios may be approximated but not directly enumerated.
20.4 Measurement requires an ordered magnitude relation.
20.4.1 If \(L_1\) exceeds \(L_2\), and \(L_2\) exceeds \(L_3\), then \(L_1\) exceeds \(L_3\).
20.4.2 Distinct lengths must stand in one direction of comparison.
20.4.3 Equality of length must be transitive.
20.4.4 Numerical measurement is meaningful only when these relations hold.
20.5 Scalar magnitudes are represented by single numbers.
20.5.1 Length, mass, temperature, density, pressure, speed, and conductivity are scalar magnitudes.
20.5.2 Velocity is a vector magnitude because it includes direction.
20.5.3 A scalar magnitude is one-dimensional as a magnitude even when its object is four-dimensional.
20.6 Congruence defines equality of magnitude.
20.6.1 Length-congruent objects share endpoint relations and constant separation when juxtaposed.
20.6.2 One object is longer than another when the latter is congruent with a proper segment of the former.
20.6.3 Without congruence, claims of equal or greater length lack truth-values.
20.6.4 Congruence may hold among objects of any dimensionality.
20.7 Standards are provisional and tested by measurement.
20.7.1 A provisional standard permits measurements of other objects.
20.7.2 Those measurements reveal physical laws.
20.7.3 The laws determine which standards yield consistent measurements.
20.7.4 A standard may be replaced when another produces greater consistency.
20.8 Triangulation extends measurement beyond direct instruments.
20.8.1 Small distances may be measured by rods or light.
20.8.2 Large distances may be measured by light when rods cannot be used.
20.8.3 Agreement within overlapping ranges establishes a common magnitude.
20.8.4 Approximate measurements support increasingly precise laws.
20.9 Metrical knowledge develops from approximation to precision.
20.9.1 Everyday experience supplies approximate measurements.
20.9.2 Approximate measurements reveal correlations.
20.9.3 Correlations enable measurements otherwise unavailable.
20.9.4 Refined measurements reveal precise physical laws.
20.10 Duration is measured through periodic processes.
20.10.1 Non-simultaneous events cannot be directly juxtaposed.
20.10.2 Their durations are compared by counting installments of a shared periodic process.
20.10.3 A valid duration standard must produce consistent measurements.
20.10.4 Convention alone cannot make an erratic process regular.
20.11 Magnitudes may be extensive or intensive.
20.11.1 An extensive magnitude exceeds the magnitude of its parts under suitable combination.
20.11.2 An additive magnitude equals the arithmetic sum of its parts under suitable combination.
20.11.3 Length and mass are extensive and approximately additive within limits.
20.11.4 Temperature and density are intensive.
20.11.5 Speed is extensive but not generally additive.
20.12 Relativity reveals the non-additivity of scalar quantities.
20.12.1 The speed of light is invariant relative to every framework.
20.12.2 Ordinary velocities do not add arithmetically in all circumstances.
20.12.3 Length and volume are consequently non-additive in general.
20.13 Direct measurement yields rational numbers.
20.13.1 Finite subdivision can determine only rational ratios.
20.13.2 Irrational magnitudes may nevertheless be inferred geometrically.
20.13.3 A diagonal may have a ratio of \(\sqrt{2}\).
20.13.4 A circumference-to-diameter ratio may be \(\pi\).
21.0 Measurement conventions are not sufficient to determine natural laws.
21.1 Metrical conventionalism treats the choice of standard as conventional.
21.1.1 A standard cannot be validated by comparison with another standard without regress.
21.1.2 Practical simplicity may favor one standard over another.
21.1.3 Practical preference does not by itself establish physical truth.
21.2 Nomic conventionalism claims that laws depend on metrical standards.
21.2.1 Measurements are relations between standards and empirical events.
21.2.2 If standards are conventional, laws may be standard-relative.
21.2.3 This does not make empirical relations themselves conventional.
21.3 Strict conventionalism is distinct from ordinary conventionalism.
21.3.1 Strict conventionalism makes laws entirely products of convention.
21.3.2 If laws were convention-determined, measurement would not be needed to identify them.
21.3.3 Measurement outcomes remain empirical even when standards are stipulated.
21.3.4 Ordinary conventionalism therefore makes laws convention-based rather than convention-determined.
21.4 Conventionalism defeats itself when standards produce causal anomalies.
21.4.1 A viable standard must yield consistent measurements.
21.4.2 Variable standards produce inconsistent measurement results.
21.4.3 Optical standards yield fewer inconsistencies than variable physical standards.
21.4.4 A standard that generates contradictions cannot represent the laws governing nature.
21.4.5 The standard that best preserves causal continuity is epistemically preferred.
22.0 Causal continuity grounds knowledge, explanation, induction, and measurement.
22.1 Causal series are continuous series.
22.1.1 Information-bearing series are continuous structure-preserving series.
22.1.2 Knowledge is produced when beliefs inherit structure through such series.
22.2 Explanations eliminate discontinuities.
22.2.1 A hypothesis is correct when it restores continuity among observed events.
22.2.2 A scientific hypothesis is explanatory when it belongs to the continuity it posits.
22.2.3 Explanation identifies causes and the structures through which they operate.
22.3 Inductive inference is justified by continuity rather than enumeration.
22.3.1 Unknown continuities are posited when observed events belong to one manifold.
22.3.2 Natural laws preserve the continuity constituting that manifold.
22.3.3 The legitimacy of induction follows from the persistence of the law-governed world.
22.4 Measurement becomes knowledge when standards preserve causal consistency.
22.4.1 A standard is acceptable when it supports coherent, repeatable relations.
22.4.2 Physical laws refine metrical practices by identifying stable continuities.
22.4.3 The elimination of causal anomalies is the ultimate constraint on convention.
